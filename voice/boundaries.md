# Boundaries — What Does Not Go In The Book

Read this before drafting anything. These are the author's own limits, captured
verbatim. They are not editorial preferences to be weighed against a good story.
**A great anecdote that violates one of these is not a great anecdote.**

---

## No photographs. No embarrassing material.

> **"No, I don't want my pictures in there. No pictures. Or anything
> embarrassing."**

<sub>Voice note 003, 2026-08-13.</sub>

- **No images of her.** Not in the manuscript, not as reference, not "just for
  the mood board."
- **Nothing embarrassing.** Her judgement of what qualifies, not yours. When
  unsure, ask before writing — never write it and offer to cut it later, because
  by then she has to be the one to object to it.

## The third guest situation — off limits

Asked about a third occasion where she needed someone to leave:

> *"I couldn't ask him to leave. I had to wait for him to… be able to leave. I
> don't want to talk about that situation."*
>
> *"No, absolutely not."*

**Do not ask about this again. Do not use it. Do not allude to it.** She declined
twice in one conversation, the second time flatly.

**But the distinction she drew from it is usable, and it matters:**

> *"That was the pure frightened. Annoyed is like Adrian."*

Two different categories with two different protocols. The book's
asking-someone-to-leave material covers **annoyed** — the extractable, quick,
no-vibe-kill problem. It must not blur that into **frightened**, where the
protocol is different, the stakes are different, and the honest advice is
security, the handler, and other people. Write the annoyed protocol well. Point
at the existence of the other category without dramatising it, and without
borrowing her story to do it.

## Sex-life details

Asked what else is off limits in print:

> **"I guess sex-life details."**

<sub>Voice note 004, 2026-08-13.</sub>

**This resolves an open flag.** *"Dick"* — her answer to what she actually wants
at 4 a.m. — stays out of the manuscript. It was funny in the room and it is a
sex-life detail, and she has now answered the question she told me to stop
asking. Recorded in `world.md`, not printed.

Same test for anything adjacent: if it would be reported as a fact about her sex
life, it doesn't go in, however good the line is.

## Substance use tied to a named person

Off limits. The book's substance material stays general, operational and
harm-reduction shaped, exactly as `references/voice.md` specifies. It never
attaches to a named friend, a named guest, or an identifiable night.

<sub>Note this cuts one detail from the Ch. 5 face-saving script: her *"probably
just too high, nervous"* was said **to** the guest as a generous excuse, not as a
report about him. Keep it as dialogue, in her mouth, framed as her handing him a
way out. Don't restate it in the narration as a fact about the man.</sub>

## Real people

Standing rules, applied throughout:

- **Change first names** for the ex (Adrian) and the neighbour (Moira).
- **No street addresses.** Not hers, not anyone's.
- **No surnames** of private individuals.
- Friends who appear warmly — Maya, Angie, Christa, Josh — still get asked
  before they're named in print. Warm portrayal is not consent.
- Material about a private person stays limited to **what they did on the night
  in question**. No general character assessments.

## The tone floor

From the original brief and reaffirmed throughout: *don't be an asshole.* The
book is bratty about standards and never cruel about people. It does not punch
down, does not mock anyone for having less money, and does not make a joke at
the expense of anyone working.

The one standing exception is the HOA complaint, and only because she asked for
it — see `book/.memoir/chapter-7.md`. Even there the neighbour's name changes.

Leave the placeholder and ask. An empty slot costs a paragraph. Publishing
