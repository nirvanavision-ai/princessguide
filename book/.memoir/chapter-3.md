**Why this is the best line in the transcript:** *in a moving vehicle while
wearing full glam* is exactly the specificity the book runs on. Nobody invents
that detail. It's funny without being told to be funny, and it's a real
operational lesson wearing a joke.

**The rule it proves:** the cabin is a controlled environment and nobody touches
your face in it. Chapter 3's seating doctrine already assigns seats before
departure — this extends it. Hands to yourself, help is offered and not
administered, and any repair that needs doing gets done stationary. It also
feeds the Communication Trio beat: the person who is a nuisance all night is a
logistics problem, and handling him is the handler's job, not yours.

**Cross-reference:** the eye/full-glam collision belongs in Chapter 6's beauty
emergencies too — a poked eye in full glam at the start of a night is a
ninety-second repair that most people would let ruin an hour.

## The driver brief — the best operational artifact in the repo

**Session:** 2026-08-13 · voice note 003. Asked what she'd say to Josh, her
driver, she produced this from memory, unprompted:

> *"Josh, we need to be there by 11, but I'm going to tell people we need to be
> there at 10:30 — half an hour before we need to get there. Please get there 45
> minutes before we need to leave. Get there earlier than we need to leave. We're
> gonna tell people it's time to leave when we still have a half hour to an hour
> left, because we're always late. Please make sure you have 1942 in the cooler
> — in the back refrigerated cooler, not an ice bucket cooler. Ew. Please pick us
> up at this time. Here is the host's phone number, so he can text me when we're
> ready to go. If you could please get us Gatorades and beef jerky, that would be
> great. We appreciate you so much. Thank you."*

**Print this almost verbatim.** It is Chapter 3 entire — every beat the spec
asks for, demonstrated instead of argued, in her own voice, in one breath.

### What's in it

**The Buffer Doctrine, and it's the chapter's best idea.** She runs *three*
staggered clocks and tells different people different times on purpose:

| Clock | Time given | Who hears it |
|---|---|---|
| Real arrival | 11:00 | Josh only |
| Announced arrival | 10:30 | the group |
| Driver on site | 45 min before departure | Josh |
| Announced departure | 30–60 min before actual | the group |

Her reason, flat and unbothered: *"because we're always late."* She doesn't
fight the lateness or moralise about it — she **builds it into the schedule as a
known constant.** That is the efficiency thesis in its purest form: don't try to
change people, absorb them into the plan. And it's funnier than a lecture about
punctuality would ever be.

**The Communication Trio, in practice.** *"Here is the host's phone number, so he
can text me when we're ready to go."* The Ch. 3 spec argues abstractly that
security, driver and host must reach *each other*. She just does it, in one
sentence, by handing over a number.

**The cooler, specified.** *"1942 in the cooler — in the back refrigerated
cooler, not an ice bucket cooler. **Ew.**"* The correction and the "Ew" both
stay. That's a Named Enemy: **The Ice Bucket Cooler.** Warm tequila is a running
theme for her — see the Ch. 4 notes on the venue that served hot 1942.

**Gatorades and beef jerky.** The real cooler contents. Not a generic "snacks."
Electrolytes and salt/protein — the 1 a.m. cooler that's worth more than the
10 p.m. cocktail, exactly as the spec claims, but with brand and item.

**The close.** *"We appreciate you so much. Thank you."* Same sign-off as the
bottle order. Every instruction she gives ends in warmth, and it's never
performed — it's one line, then done. See Ch. 5 on not being extra.
