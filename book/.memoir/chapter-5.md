## Author instruction — a new required beat

> **"One of those things should be, like, how to politely ask someone to leave.
> That's a good tip that I've just learned this year."**

She asked for this directly. It becomes the chapter's second signature argument,
alongside how to stop thanking someone.

## The doctrine, in her words

> **"Okay, I have a problem. I have to take care of it. It's distracting. Let's
> extract the problem and get back to the party. Quick and efficient. No vibe
> kill."**

That is the whole protocol and it should be printed close to verbatim. Note what
it is *not*: it isn't a confrontation, a lesson, or a moment. **The goal is not
justice, it's the return to the party.** Every choice downstream — private not
public, brief not thorough, face-saving not accurate — follows from optimising
for *time back at the party* rather than for being understood.

Which is also the efficiency thesis, applied to a person: the problem is
friction, friction is stolen time, remove it and get back to the good part.

## The taxonomy — and it matters

> *"That was the pure frightened. Annoyed is like Adrian."*

**Two categories, two protocols.** The chapter covers **annoyed**. See
[`voice/boundaries.md`](../../voice/boundaries.md) — the frightened case is off
limits, and the material must not blur them together.

## Case one — someone you're close to

> *"I asked to speak to them privately."*
>
> *"He was like, hey, can I talk to you for a minute? I was like, hey, I just
> don't… it's not you, it's me. We're just not really vibing. We haven't seen
> each other [since the] 1st group hang."*

**Private, always.** Ask for the minute rather than announcing anything. The
script is deliberately soft and slightly untrue, and that's the point — you are
not building a case, you're providing an exit.

## Case two — someone another guest brought

> *"Since she brought him, I asked her to get [him to leave], because he was
> making me and another girl uncomfortable."*
>
> *"Well, she brought him, that's why."*

**The rule: whoever brought them removes them.** Not you, not security, not the
handler — the inviter. It's cleaner, it's faster, and it puts the awkwardness
with the person who created it. This is the concrete, non-dramatic version of
*have someone be an asshole for you*: it isn't about outsourcing cruelty, it's
about correct ownership.

Her framing to the guest, which is a masterclass in face-saving:

> *"Probably just too high, nervous, but we'd like to lie down for the night
> anyways. So probably be a good idea to go."*

**Nobody is accused of anything.** She hands him a generous explanation for his
own behaviour (*too high, nervous*) and a reason for leaving that isn't about
him at all (*we'd like to lie down anyway*). He gets to leave with his dignity,
which is precisely what makes him leave quickly. An accurate accusation would
have started a conversation. That's a vibe kill.

### The tells, and the threshold

> *"The vibe is off, he's killing it. I don't like the sound of his voice. I
> don't like him forcing conversation."*
>
> *"When she got a little sleepy and he was like, 'you get 5 minutes to sleep.'
> He kept saying that."*
>
> *"I just could not think while he was talking."*
>
> *"We were basically hiding in the room all night."*

**The diagnostic the chapter should hand the reader is the last one.** If you are
hiding in a room in your own home, the decision has already been made and you
are just paying interest on it. That's the threshold — not the moment someone is
rude, but the moment the room reorganises itself around avoiding them.

And the tell inside the tell: the moment she names as when the vibe truly went is
not an insult or a scene. It's a man telling a sleepy woman *"you get 5 minutes
to sleep,"* repeatedly. Small, controlling, in someone else's house. Worth
writing carefully — this is the chapter showing a reader what to notice, and it
is more useful than any story about someone being loud.

<sub>*"I never want to date a guy with an Australian accent, ever."* Recorded
because it's funny, flagged because it's the one line here that punches at a
category of person rather than a behaviour. Her call.</sub>

## The Scarlett's ethic — for the Staff Doctrine

> *"Maya's 1st night at Scarlett's was so much fun. I think she learned to
> appreciate beautiful women that can dance that are friendly and are fun at a
> strip club. **I love taking my friends there, and just making sure the girls
> that are working have a great night, and they make money easily and
> happily.**"*

This is the warmest thing in the whole knowledge base and it belongs in the
Staff Doctrine section, which is currently written as *tipping is
infrastructure* — true, and colder than she actually is.

**"Making sure the girls that are working have a great night"** inverts the
usual relationship completely. She's not a customer being served well; she's
taken responsibility for the *staff's* night. That is host doctrine extended to
people who aren't her guests, and it's the same instinct as the driver, the
bottle order, and the friends' feet.

*"Easily and happily"* is the load-bearing phrase — not just that they earn, but
that earning it isn't a grind that night.

**Note:** this makes Scarlett's a strip club, which the earlier notes didn't
establish. Ask before it goes in print — it changes what kind of book this reads
as, and that's her decision, not mine.
