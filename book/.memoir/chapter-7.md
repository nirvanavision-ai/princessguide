## Why this is much better than the version in the voice note

Your telling is *"I was on FaceTime with family, I went outside and couldn't
hear a thing."* Her telling is a dated log running April through June with
recordings attached and the phrase *"every single night now."*

**Both are true and the gap between them is the whole joke.** The defensive
version — *she was wrong, I was on the phone* — is a lesser story and readers
will smell it. The honest version is that you were having the year of your life
and someone downstairs was keeping a spreadsheet about it, and that is genuinely
funny in a way the defensive version cannot be.

It also gives Chapter 7 real stakes instead of a hypothetical. The chapter
currently warns that *the after-party that ends in a complaint ends the venue,
and the venue is your home.* You have a documented HOA case with a paper trail
and audio. That's not a cautionary hypothetical any more, it's a live one, and a
book that admits it is a book worth trusting.

**The gift in it:** *"like living next door to a nightclub!"* Moira wrote the
best line in the chapter and she meant it as an accusation. Print it as a review.

**The rule the chapter extracts:** quiet hours are a hard operational constraint
like a set time or a bag policy — not a suggestion you can be right about. Being
right doesn't get your building back. The after-party's real ceiling isn't the
guest list, it's 10 p.m. to 8 a.m. at 5 dB, and every protocol in the chapter
(the pre-flight, the pre-set volumes, the deceleration playlist, the pre-booked

## A: — the comedown

> "the Jay Z was such a peak moment. Like we went so high there at that concert,
> we felt so hard… the vibes were off… we got so high, you gotta come down. They
> are. Yeah. That's just fucked up."

Recovery is currently written as logistics — water, makeup off, shoes elevated.
This is the emotional half nobody writes down: the better the peak, the harder
the landing. The comedown is an event you can plan for rather than be ambushed
by. *"You gotta come down. That's just fucked up"* said flat is the whole idea.

## Privacy note — flagging once, not advising

The letter names a real private individual, gives her role, and carries two
street addresses including your own. That's fine in a working notebook and it is
**not** fine in a published book. Standard handling: change the neighbour's name,
drop the addresses, keep the HOA structure, the dates and the quotes. The story
loses nothing — *"the HOA director downstairs"* is funnier than a surname anyway.

Reproducing a complaint made against you is ordinary memoir and entirely your
call. Publishing an identifiable private neighbour's name and address alongside
it is a different thing, and it's the kind of detail a publisher's legal read
would catch late and expensively. Easier to decide now.
