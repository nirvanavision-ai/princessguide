# Exemplar Prose — Calibration Only

Not a chapter. Not for publication. This is the target sentence rhythm, the
deadpan timing, and the ratio of attitude to actual operational content.

**What this does not yet demonstrate**, and what `voice/samples.md` will teach
better than any invented sample can:

- The **babe/sis/girl** address. These passages are unaddressed; the real prose
  talks *to* someone.
- The **host energy** — she's doing this so her friends have a great night, and
  that warmth should be audible under the rigor.
- **Music as the reason.** The thing she actually goes out for is missing here
  entirely.

Treat the rhythm below as right and the temperature as incomplete. The moment
real samples land, they win.

---

## The Two-Lap Test

There is a moment, roughly ninety minutes before the car, when a woman puts on
a shoe she bought specifically for tonight and decides — against every signal
her body is sending her — that it's fine.

It is not fine. It has never once been fine.

So we test. Shoes on, fully dressed, and you walk your apartment. Not a shuffle
to the mirror. A lap: hallway, kitchen, back through the living room, around
whatever your version of the coffee table is. That's one. Then you do it again.

If the second lap hurts, the shoe does not come.

This is not a preference. It's arithmetic. A club night is somewhere between
four and nine thousand steps, a meaningful percentage of them on a floor with
the traction of a fresh oyster, and at least two hundred of them at speed
because someone has said the words "they're closing the list." Your apartment
is flat, carpeted, and eleven degrees cooler than the room you're going to. If
the shoe is losing in the easiest conditions it will ever face, it is not going
to stage a comeback at 1 a.m. in a coat check line.

There is no third lap. The third lap is denial with cardio.

**The verdict:** two laps, clean, or the backups are already in the car.

## On Not Sitting in the Front

I want to be precise about this, because people think it's snobbery and it is
actually blocking and staging.

The front seat is the handler's seat, the driver's seat, or nobody's. When the
SUV pulls up, the rear passenger door opens curbside — which is the side with
the venue, the door team, the line, and every phone in it. You step out into the
room. The front seat puts you out into traffic, into a blind spot, and into a
half-second of climbing down that no one has ever made look good.

The exit itself is three movements and it is worth practicing exactly once, in
your own driveway, like a normal person: bag first, then both feet to the
pavement together, then stand. Both feet. Not one, then a hunt for the second.
And never a hand on the doorframe — the doorframe is for people who are
surprised to be arriving.

Assign the seats before departure, in the group thread, by name. Yes, in
writing. The alternative is four adults negotiating a vehicle on a curb in
November, which is not a scene, it's a delay, and delays are how the host gives
your table's arrival window to somebody else.

**The verdict:** rear passenger side, bag first, both feet, no doorframe.

## Why this works

- The joke and the logistics are **the same sentence**. The comedy comes from
  treating an absurd standard with total operational seriousness.
- Numbers do the heavy lifting: two laps, nine thousand steps, three movements,
  eleven degrees. Specificity reads as authority and lands as humor.
- Rhythm: long sentence, long sentence, short sentence, fragment. Then a rule
  on its own line.
- Named Enemies recur in caps-lock spirit: The Third Lap, The Doorframe.
- No motivational uplift. No "queen." No explaining the joke afterward.
- Every section closes on a verdict, never on a summary of itself.
